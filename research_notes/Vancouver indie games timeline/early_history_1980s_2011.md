# Early history of the Vancouver game industry and indie scene (1980s–2011): fact-check of draft timeline

Scope: the "AAA roots (1980s–2000s)" and "First indies (2008–2011)" sections of the draft timeline. Each entry gets a verdict, the corrected facts, and canonical replacement links. Unless noted otherwise, every URL below was opened during this research. Three URLs could not be opened (403 errors or blocked) and are marked NOT OPENED: nytimes.com, mobygames.com, and pressreader.com. web.archive.org was also blocked for this researcher.

## Q1. Distinctive Software → EA Canada → EA Vancouver (draft entry "1982–1991")

### Takeaway
**Verdict: mostly confirmed, with corrections.**
- Mattrick and Sember founded Distinctive Software in Burnaby in 1982. Wikipedia's EA Vancouver lead says it "opened" in January 1983.
- Mattrick was 17. No source gives Sember's age, so the draft should not imply an age for him.
- EA's purchase was reported by the NYT on June 18, 1991. Wikipedia puts closing in July 1991 (pooling of interests). The price was US$10M or $11M, depending on the Wikipedia section.
- The renamed company was "Electronic Arts Canada" / EA Canada.
- The "largest and oldest" studio claim is uncited on Wikipedia. Drop it or soften it, e.g. "one of EA's largest studios, about 1,300 staff in 2013." EA itself was founded in 1982 in California, so "oldest" is dubious.

### Cited Findings
- Mattrick was born February 13, 1964. He "co-founded Distinctive Software (DSI) in 1982 in Vancouver at age 17" with Jeff Sember. EA's acquisition was "accomplished through a pooling of interest transaction in July 1991." — [Wikipedia: Don Mattrick](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Don_Mattrick)
- The founding date conflicts within Wikipedia. The EA Vancouver lead says the studio "opened as Distinctive Software in January 1983," but the infobox says 1982. The 1982 date ties to Sydney Development Corp. agreeing to publish *Evolution*. "Mattrick (age 17) and Jeff Sember approached Sydney Development Corporation." — [Wikipedia: EA Vancouver](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/EA_Vancouver); [Wikipedia: Distinctive Software](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Distinctive_Software)
- The price conflicts within Wikipedia. The EA Vancouver History section says EA "acquired Distinctive Software in 1991 for $11 million." The Distinctive Software section says the deal was "worth US$10 million," citing "COMPANY NEWS; Electronic Arts To Buy Distinctive," *New York Times*, June 18, 1991. — [Wikipedia: Distinctive Software](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Distinctive_Software); NYT primary source: https://www.nytimes.com/1991/06/18/business/company-news-electronic-arts-to-buy-distinctive.html (NOT OPENED: the fetch tool is blocked from nytimes.com, so the price is unconfirmed against the primary source)
- After the purchase, Distinctive was renamed "EA Canada." The former names in the infobox are EA Burnaby and EA Canada. The studio is "located in Burnaby, British Columbia." — [Wikipedia: EA Vancouver](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/EA_Vancouver)
- The renamed subsidiary was "Electronic Arts Canada Inc." — [Encyclopedia.com: Electronic Arts Inc. company history](https://www.encyclopedia.com/books/politics-and-business-magazines/electronic-arts-inc) (seen only as a search snippet)
- Wikipedia's lead says EA Vancouver "is also Electronic Arts's largest and oldest studio," with **no inline citation**. The page carries a "needs more citations" banner. The size claim is "approximately 1,300 people" plus "the world's largest video game test operation," citing a 2013 EA jobs page. — [Wikipedia: EA Vancouver](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/EA_Vancouver)
- The Georgia Straight's "family tree" says Mattrick and Sember founded DSI with help from Tarrnie Williams to license *Evolution*, and that EA bought it in 1991 and it became EA Canada. — [Georgia Straight, Blaine Kyllo, "Vancouver's video game family tree," Jan 28, 2009](https://www.straight.com/article-198534/video-game-family-tree)
- Additional source cited by Wikipedia: Jacob Zinn, "EA Canada pushes boundaries in Burnaby," *Burnaby Now*, Sept 5, 2014. NOT OPENED.

### Inferences
- Suggested wording: "1982: Don Mattrick (17) and Jeff Sember found Distinctive Software in Burnaby. June 1991: EA agrees to buy it (about US$10–11M), and it becomes EA Canada, later EA Vancouver."
- "Oldest" is likely false in a strict sense. EA was founded in 1982 in California, and its California studios date to that founding. DSI joined EA only in 1991. It is better to say "EA's largest studio and its oldest acquired studio still operating." Even that is unverified, so the safest wording is "one of EA's largest studios."
- Link replacements: replace cavac.at with https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Don_Mattrick, and wiki.krisyotam.com with https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/EA_Vancouver plus https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Distinctive_Software. The MobyGames link https://www.mobygames.com/company/24/ returned 403 to the fetch tool. Search results show it as Distinctive Software in Vancouver through 1991, but it could not be opened.

### Gaps
- The exact price (US$10M vs $11M) needs the NYT article, which could not be opened.
- No date was found for the switch from "EA Canada" to "EA Vancouver." Wikipedia doesn't give one.
- Sember's age was not found anywhere.

## Q2. 1990s studios: Radical, Relic, Barking Dog/Rockstar Vancouver, and other seed studios (draft entry "1990s")

### Takeaway
**Verdict: confirmed, but the draft is too vague.** Exact dates are available for each studio:
- Radical: September 1991.
- Relic: May 1997.
- Barking Dog: May 1998, founded by six ex-Radical developers. Take-Two acquired it on August 1, 2002, and it became Rockstar Vancouver.
- Black Box Games: 1998, founded by ex-Radical staff in Burnaby. EA bought it in June 2002.

The Rockstar Vancouver link should go to en.wikipedia.org. The 2000s generation of studios (below) belongs in the timeline as the pipeline that later fed indie talent.

### Cited Findings
- **Radical Entertainment:** founded September 1991 by Rory Armes, Dave Davis and Ian Wilkinson, originally in Yaletown. Vivendi acquired it in 2005. On June 28, 2012, Activision announced a "significant reduction in staff," after which Radical became a support studio. — [Wikipedia: Radical Entertainment](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Radical_Entertainment)
- The Straight says Radical was created after the 1991 EA deal by Ian Wilkinson and DSI's Rory Armes. — [Georgia Straight family tree](https://www.straight.com/article-198534/video-game-family-tree)
- **Relic Entertainment:**
  - Founded May 1997 in Vancouver by Alex Garden, Luke Moloney, Erin Daly, Aaron Kambeitz, Rob Cunningham, Gary Shaw and Shane Alfreds.
  - *Homeworld* released September 28, 1999.
  - THQ announced the acquisition April 27, 2004, for about US$10M cash.
  - Source: [Wikipedia: Relic Entertainment](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Relic_Entertainment)
  - The Straight names only Garden and Moloney, "from EA Canada/Radical." — [Georgia Straight family tree](https://www.straight.com/article-198534/video-game-family-tree)
- **Barking Dog Studios / Rockstar Vancouver:**
  - "The sextet established Barking Dog in May 1998": Glenn Barnes, Peter Grant, Michael Gyori, Christopher Mair, Brian Thalken and Sean Thompson, all ex-Radical.
  - Take-Two announced the acquisition August 1, 2002, for US$3M cash, and the studio became Rockstar Vancouver.
  - Its closure and merger into Rockstar Toronto were announced July 9, 2012.
  - Source: [Wikipedia: Rockstar Vancouver](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Rockstar_Vancouver)
- **Black Box Games:** founded 1998 by ex-Radical staff in Burnaby. EA acquired it in June 2002, during *Need for Speed: Hot Pursuit 2*, and renamed it EA Black Box. It was renamed Quicklime Games in July 2012 and shut down in April 2013. — [Wikipedia: EA Black Box](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/EA_Black_Box)
- **Next Level Games:** founded August 22, 2002 by Eric Randall, Douglas Tronsgard, Jason Carr and David Catlin, all ex-EA Black Box. Nintendo announced the acquisition January 2021, effective March 31, 2021. — [Wikipedia: Next Level Games](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Next_Level_Games)
- **Blue Castle Games:** founded July 4, 2004 in Burnaby (founder Robert Barrett). Capcom acquired it after *Dead Rising 2* (2010), and it became Capcom Vancouver. Its closure was announced September 18, 2018. — [Wikipedia: Capcom Vancouver](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Capcom_Vancouver)
- **Propaganda Games:** founded April 2005 as Revolution Interactive by Josh Holmes, Howard Donaldson, Daryl Anselmo and Jorge Freitas. Disney Interactive acquired it in 2005. Its games were *Turok* (2008) and *Tron: Evolution* (2010), and it closed January 2011. — [Wikipedia: Propaganda Games](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Propaganda_Games)
- **Slant Six Games:** founded 2005 in Vancouver (key person Brian Thalken, a Barking Dog co-founder). It closed in 2013, soon after *Galactic Reign* shut down in August 2013. — [Wikipedia: Slant Six Games](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Slant_Six_Games)
- **Hothead Games:** founded 2006 by ex-Radical staff Steve Bocska, Vlad Ceraldi and Joel DeYoung. Its first games were the *Penny Arcade Adventures* episodes (2008). — [Wikipedia: Hothead Games](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Hothead_Games). The studio cut staff in December 2008 after failing to find a publisher for *Swarm*. — [Georgia Straight family tree](https://www.straight.com/article-198534/video-game-family-tree)
- **United Front Games:**
  - Founded 2007 by former members of EA Black Box, Rockstar Vancouver, Radical and Volition.
  - Its games were *ModNation Racers* (2010) and *Sleeping Dogs* (2012), and its closure was announced October 17, 2016.
  - Source: [Wikipedia: United Front Games](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/United_Front_Games)
  - The Straight says about 10 founders, with first hires from Rockstar, EA, Radical, Slant Six, Action Pants, Propaganda, Blue Castle and Next Level. — [Georgia Straight family tree](https://www.straight.com/article-198534/video-game-family-tree)
- **Backbone Entertainment (Foundation 9):** cut most of its Vancouver staff in September 2008 and kept a satellite operation. — [Georgia Straight family tree](https://www.straight.com/article-198534/video-game-family-tree)
- "Pandemic Vancouver": no source found. The Straight family tree does not mention Pandemic. Pandemic's second studio was in Brisbane, Australia, so this may be a confusion. Leave it out unless an interviewee confirms it.

### Inferences
- The key lineage is DSI/EA → Radical (1991) → Relic (1997), Barking Dog and Black Box (both 1998) → Next Level (2002) → Hothead (2006) and United Front (2007). This is the "spin-off" growth pattern the Straight describes: "That established what would become the pattern for growth in this city."
- Suggested replacement entries:
  - "Sep 1991: Radical founded."
  - "May 1997: Relic founded. Sep 28, 1999: *Homeworld*."
  - "May 1998: Barking Dog and Black Box founded by ex-Radical staff."
  - "2002: EA buys Black Box (June), Take-Two buys Barking Dog (Aug 1), Next Level founded (Aug 22)."
  - "2004–2007: Blue Castle, Propaganda, Slant Six, Hothead, United Front."

### Gaps
- No founding date was found for Action Pants (later Ubisoft Vancouver), or for Backbone's Vancouver office (which grew out of Digital Eclipse Vancouver). These were not researched in depth.
- No primary press releases were opened. The founding dates above come from canonical Wikipedia and the Straight.

## Q3. Klei Entertainment founding (draft entry "2005")

### Takeaway
**Verdict: confirmed, with additions.**
- Jamie Cheng founded Klei in July 2005 in Vancouver after working at Relic as an AI programmer. He bootstrapped it, reportedly funded by selling THQ shares and a $10,000 loan from his brother.
- Wikipedia names Jeffrey Agala as a co-founder and Creative Director. No source found calls Kevin Forbes a co-founder: he was an early designer and co-created the *Don't Starve* prototype.
- The first game, *Eets*, came out March 27, 2006 for Windows.

### Cited Findings
- Klei was founded in July 2005 by Jamie Cheng, a former "specialist AI programmer" at Relic. Jeffrey Agala became "co-founder and Creative Director." *Eets* came out March 27, 2006 on Windows. Kevin Forbes is not mentioned as a founder. — [Wikipedia: Klei Entertainment](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Klei_Entertainment)
- "Founder Jamie Cheng bootstrapped his independent studio after leaving Relic Entertainment." No founding year is given. — [Georgia Straight, "BOV 2013: Here's to the video games made in Vancouver," Sept 18, 2013](https://www.straight.com/life/425486/bov-2013-heres-video-games-made-vancouver)
- From search snippets of Wikipedia-derived pages, not independently verified:
  - Cheng funded Klei by selling THQ shares and borrowing $10,000 from his brother.
  - Kevin Forbes and Ju-Lian Kwan made the prototype that became *Don't Starve*.
  - Source caveat: [Klei, Wikipedia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Klei_Entertainment)
- Klei also passed on making *N+* for Metanet, then shipped *Shank* (2010). — [CGM: History of the Canadian Videogame Scene, Part 2](https://www.cgmagonline.com/articles/features/history-canadian-videogame-scene-part-2/)

### Inferences
- Suggested wording: "Jul 2005: Jamie Cheng, an ex-Relic AI programmer, bootstraps Klei Entertainment. Mar 27, 2006: first game, *Eets* (PC)." Klei is arguably the first notable Vancouver indie studio, which partly fills the draft's gap "the indie scene before 2008."
- Cheng worked at Relic after the 2004 THQ acquisition, which is consistent with him owning THQ shares.

### Gaps
- No primary interview confirming the THQ-shares and brother-loan story was opened.
- Kevin Forbes's role (co-founder or not) has no primary source. Treat him as an early designer unless interviews say otherwise.

## Q5. Fantastic Contraption and the Northways (draft entry "2008")

### Takeaway
**Verdict: confirmed, with a precise date and a geography caveat.**
- The Flash version of *Fantastic Contraption* was released September 16, 2008.
- Colin Northway made it on the side while working as a web programmer (web work for lawyers). He had no professional game experience. It drew over 3.5M unique users in about 3 months, and he sold the rights to inXile, which released iOS on Jan 26, 2009.
- **Geography:** the Northways met at the University of Victoria. After the game, they travelled the world for about five years and only settled in Vancouver in 2014. So this is a BC/Victoria-rooted milestone, not strictly a Vancouver one.

### Cited Findings
- Flash release September 16, 2008, by "Canadian indie developer Colin Northway." The rights were sold to inXile, with iOS on January 26, 2009. *Fantastic Contraption 2* came out July 27, 2010. — [Wikipedia: Fantastic Contraption (2008 video game)](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Fantastic_Contraption_(2008_video_game))
- The game was made on the side while Colin did web work for lawyers. It "blew up," and the couple quit their jobs. They began making games while at the University of Victoria. After the game they travelled for about five years, and "In 2014, the couple decided to settle down somewhere for good," choosing Vancouver. — [Georgia Straight, V. S. Wells, "How Vancouver became a hub for indie video games," Mar 27, 2024](https://www.straight.com/node/1442795) (same article as https://www.straight.com/tech/how-vancouver-became-a-hub-for-indie-video-games)
- At the GDC Austin Independent Games Summit (Sept 2009), Northway described building the game without professional experience, in a few weeks, with notepad and a command-line compiler. It drew over 3.5M unique users in the first three months or so. — [Game Developer: "GDC Austin: How Fantastic Contraption Became a Fantastic Hit"](https://www.gamedeveloper.com/pc/gdc-austin-how-i-fantastic-contraption-i-became-a-fantastic-hit) (seen only via a search snippet, Simon Carless, Sept 15, 2009). GDC Vault talk: https://gdcvault.com/play/1012296/Postmortem-The-Design-Business-Behind (not opened)

### Inferences
- Suggested wording: "Sep 16, 2008: Colin Northway (UVic grad, then a web programmer) releases *Fantastic Contraption* in Flash. The Northways are not yet Vancouver-based."
- Where Colin lived in 2008 (Victoria or Vancouver) was not established. Ask in interviews.

### Gaps
- No source found states Colin's city of residence in 2008.

## Q8. 2009–2011 layoffs, mobile/social shift, Montreal overtaking Vancouver (draft entry "2009–2011")

### Takeaway
**Verdict: confirmed, but the link is mis-dated, and figures can be added.**
- The Straight's "Vancouver video-game industry in transition" (Aug 31, 2011) quotes ESAC's Julien Lavoie: "Montreal has taken the lead when it comes to employment."
- ESAC's May 30, 2011 release, based on a **Secor Consulting** report (not Nordicity), puts national employment at about 16,000 across nearly 350 companies, with Quebec holding over half the jobs.
- ESAC data cited by other outlets put British Columbia at 3,882 employees in 83 companies (2011 data).
- Concrete closures: Propaganda in January 2011, *True Crime: Hong Kong* cancelled. In the 2008 downturn, Backbone cut most Vancouver staff (September 2008) and Hothead cut staff (December 2008).

### Cited Findings
- Larger Vancouver studios had been cutting staff for a couple of years. The Propaganda closure and the *True Crime: Hong Kong* cancellation reflected a decline in console work. Lavoie (ESAC): "Montreal has taken the lead when it comes to employment." Over half of Canadian sector jobs are in Quebec. East Side Games CEO Jason Bailey: "The real money and momentum and excitement in the industry is around social and mobile." — [Georgia Straight, Aug 31, 2011](https://www.straight.com/node/24485)
- ESAC, "Canadian Video Game Industry Shows Sustained Growth," May 30, 2011: about 16,000 jobs, nearly 350 companies, Quebec over half of jobs. It is based on a Secor Consulting report. No BC figure is given. — [ESAC](https://theesa.ca/?p=2077)
- BC had 3,882 employees and 83 companies in the 2011 figures. In 2013, BC had 5,150 employees, 31.2% of national employment, behind Quebec at 53.0%. — search snippets for [MCV UK](https://mcvuk.com/?p=80314) and [House of Commons INDU report](https://ourcommons.ca/documentviewer/en/41-2/INDU/report-4/page-30) (NOT OPENED, snippets only)
- Propaganda Games closed January 2011. — [Wikipedia: Propaganda Games](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Propaganda_Games)
- Backbone cut most Vancouver staff in September 2008, and Hothead cut staff in December 2008. — [Georgia Straight family tree](https://www.straight.com/article-198534/video-game-family-tree)

### Inferences
- The timeline could add: "Jan 2011: Disney closes Propaganda Games" and "Aug 31, 2011: Straight reports Montreal has overtaken Vancouver; ESAC/Secor puts over half of Canada's ~16,000 game jobs in Quebec."
- The 3,882 figure is for all of BC, not just Vancouver.

### Gaps

## Q9. Full Indie origins (draft entries "May 2010" and "2011")

### Takeaway
**Verdict: confirmed, with precise dates.**
- Jake Birkett proposed a regular Vancouver game-dev meetup on his blog on April 24, 2010, and Alex Vostrov replied the same day. On May 5, 2010, Birkett wrote that "Alex and I have got the ball rolling," and by May 10, 2010 they had set up the meetup.com group "Vancouver Indie Game Developers" and scheduled the first meeting.
- Birkett had moved from the UK to Vancouver to work for Big Fish Games, and Vostrov ran Rocket Bear Games (formerly Indiebird).
- CGM says the group rebranded as **Full Indie in March 2011** and moved to a venue for about 100 people.
- The first Full Indie Game Jam was in **June 2011** (weekend of June 17) at the Centre for Digital Media. This is from Village Gamer search snippets only.
- Vancouver held Global Game Jams in 2009 (SFU Harbour Centre, 26 entrants) and 2010 (UBC, run by Kimberly Voll), before Full Indie's own jam.
- **Victoria caveat:** Sarah Northway said a former Victoria roommate "started a meet-up in Victoria for indie devs" and "brought it with him" to Vancouver. The Straight treats this as Full Indie's origin. It sits awkwardly with Birkett, a UK expat at Big Fish Vancouver, being the documented founder. The roommate is probably Vostrov, but this is unconfirmed.

### Cited Findings
- Blog post "Game Industry Meetups in Vancouver," Apr 24, 2010: "I'd like to start up a Game Industry meetup that is regular and very useful for Game Devs." Alex commented the same day ("I think that this is a great idea"). May 5, 2010: "Alex and I have got the ball rolling on this." May 10, 2010: "We setup a meetup.com group and have scheduled our first meeting"; the group was named Vancouver Indie Game Developers. — [Grey Alien Games blog (Jake Birkett)](https://greyaliengames.com/blog/game-industry-meetups-in-vancouver/)
- Big Fish Games moved Birkett and his family from the UK to Vancouver, where he worked two years. By Nov 30, 2012, Full Indie had "well over 1000 members and monthly meetups with over 100 people." — [Grey Alien Games, "My Game Dev Story," Nov 30, 2012](https://greyaliengames.com/blog/?p=1044)
- Alex Vostrov: "I'm from Vancouver, Canada," the one-man Rocket Bear Games (formerly Indiebird), July 19, 2010. — [Game Developer](https://www.gamedeveloper.com/game-platforms/interview-a-rocket-bear-takes-off---vostrov-on-going-full-time-indie)
- Wikipedia confirms the founders (Birkett and Vostrov) and the May 2010 start as monthly meetups, and says the first Full Indie Summit was in 2013. It gives no date for the rebrand or first jam. — [Wikipedia: Full Indie](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Full_Indie)
- CGM, about December 2014 (no byline or date on the page):
  - "Vancouver Indie Game Developers" started May 2010 as a monthly social meetup.
  - In **March 2011** it rebranded as Full Indie and moved to a venue for about 100 people.
  - An annual Full Indie game jam started in 2011, after the first two Vancouver Global Game Jams proved popular.
  - Source: [CGM Part 2](https://www.cgmagonline.com/articles/features/history-canadian-videogame-scene-part-2/)
- Founded 2010 by Birkett and Vostrov, with a peak of 5,000 members. Sarah Northway: their former Victoria roommate "started a meet-up in Victoria for indie devs" and "brought it with him," and the Victoria community was "just this really small community in 2010." — [Georgia Straight, Mar 27, 2024](https://www.straight.com/node/1442795). The PressReader copy returned 403 (NOT OPENED). Use the straight.com URL instead.
- First Full Indie Game Jam: "first ever Game Jam this month at the Centre for Digital Media campus," running "the June 17th weekend," with 30 spots. — Village Gamer, June 4, 2011 (search snippet only; the print page https://villagegamer.net/2011/06/04/full-indie-game-jam/print had no body and showed a misleading 2014 timestamp). Vostrov in Vancouver Weekly 2011: "Full Indie does a jam too… It's cheaper and it's also a lot easier to do a jam that's small." — [Vancouver Weekly](https://vancouverweekly.com/?p=5749) (snippet only, NOT OPENED)
- Global Game Jam 2009 Vancouver (Jan 30–Feb 1, 2009) was held at SFU Harbour Centre, organized via IGDA, with 26 entrants. — [Village Gamer, Feb 3, 2009](https://www.villagegamer.net/2009/02/03/global-game-jam-vancouver-creativity-in-a-box/)
- Kimberly Voll (Centre for Digital Media, Great Northern Way) has run GGJ Vancouver since 2010. The 2013 edition was the fifth, at UBC Life Sciences Centre, with about 250 participants. — [Georgia Straight, Jan 24, 2013](https://www.straight.com/life/345091/global-game-jam-enthusiasm-keeps-growing)
- Birkett's team made a GGJ 2011 game, "Invasion of the Giant Bacteria." — [Grey Alien Games blog](https://greyaliengames.com/blog/invasion-of-the-giant-bacteria-our-ggj-2011-game/) (search result only, NOT OPENED)

### Inferences
- Suggested entries:
  - "Jan 30–Feb 1, 2009: first Global Game Jam, Vancouver site at SFU Harbour Centre."
  - "Apr 24 – May 10, 2010: Jake Birkett (Big Fish Games, a UK transplant) and Alex Vostrov (Rocket Bear Games) launch the Vancouver Indie Game Developers meetup."
